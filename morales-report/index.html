“Resubmitting” a restraining order is really three different things in New York, and each one uses a different form. Pick the one that matches your situation — the steps and the law change depending on which it is.

First filing — asking Family Court for an Order of Protection

💲

Cost: $0. No filing fee in Family Court, no lawyer required, and service of the papers is free. You can ask the judge for a free assigned lawyer if you can't afford one.

The law, simply

Family offense cases live in Family Court Act Article 8 (§§ 812–846). You file a sworn paper called a petition saying the other person committed a “family offense” — one of the specific acts listed in § 812 (hitting, threatening, stalking, repeated harassing contact, and more — full list in “The law” section below). Family Court is a civil court: at the hearing you must show it is “more likely than not” that it happened (§ 832, called a fair preponderance of the evidence). You do not have to call the police first or press charges to use Family Court.

The form

Form 8-2  Family Offense Petition. Get it free at the courthouse (staff will hand it to you and can help you fill it in), or download it from the official NY Courts family offense forms page linked at the bottom of this page.

  2. Fill in Form 8-2 at home from your worksheet, or at the courthouse with staff help. Both are fine and both are free.
  3. File it at Monroe County Family Court (in person or online — full instructions in “How to file” below). Same-day matters: go in the morning if you can.
  4. See the judge the same day for a possible Temporary Order (§ 828) — the judge can grant one right away, without the other person there.
  5. The papers get served on the other person for free. The order counts once they've been served (handed the papers).
  6. Come back for the hearing on the date the court gives you. Bring your evidence and any witnesses.

Extend it — your order is still active but the end date is coming

💲

Cost: $0. Asking to extend is free — no fee, no lawyer required, service is free. File before the expiration date printed on your order; an expired Family Court order generally can't be revived, and a Criminal Court order can't be extended at all once it ends.

The law, simply

Family Court Act § 842 says the court may extend an order “for a reasonable period of time upon a showing of good cause” or if both sides agree. Good cause does not require new abuse or a new violation. The law says it word-for-word: the fact that no abuse happened while the order was in effect “shall not, in itself” be a reason to deny an extension — that's often proof the order is working. Judges look at the whole picture: why the order was issued, your relationship now, any contact or violations since, and whether your fear for your safety is reasonable. The judge must state the reason for the decision on the record.

The forms

GF-10 Notice of Motion for Extension or Modification of Order of Protection  +  GF-10a Affirmation in Support — your sworn statement explaining the good cause. A “motion” just means a formal written request to the same court that issued your order.

What GF-10 / GF-10a ask for

Steps

  1. Find the expiration date on your order and start at least 2–4 weeks before it.
  2. Write your good-cause story: why the order was granted, anything that's happened since (even “small” contact counts — log it), and why you still need protection.
  3. Complete GF-10 + GF-10a and file them with the Family Court that issued the order (in person or EDDS — see “How to file”).
  4. Have the motion served on the other person — ask the clerk about free service options.
  5. Go to the court date. Your old order stays in effect until the judge decides. Bring the order and your log.
Your next step: check the date on your order today. If it's within 30 days of expiring, treat this as urgent and start the worksheet now.

Re-file — your order expired, or your earlier case was dismissed

💲

Cost: $0. A new petition is free, just like the first one — no fee, no lawyer required, service is free. There is no charge for “starting over.”

The law, simply

Once an order expires, it can't be extended — so you file a new family offense petition under Article 8, using the same Form 8-2. The judge will need a family offense (§ 812) to act on, so a new petition is strongest when it includes recent incidents, new threats or contact, or an ongoing pattern — and you can (and should) describe the earlier history and the prior order as background. If your earlier case was dismissed because you missed a court date, tell the clerk — say so in the petition and ask about your options when you file.

The form

Form 8-2 Family Offense Petition — the same form as a first filing. Write “prior order” details (dates, docket number if you have it) in the background section, and lead with the newest incidents.

Steps

  1. Log anything new since the order ended — texts, showing up, threats, contact through other people. Dates matter.
  2. Gather the old paperwork if you have it: the expired order or dismissal. Note the docket number. (No paperwork? File anyway — the court has its own records.)
  3. Complete a fresh Form 8-2 from your worksheet: newest incidents first, then a short history (“An order was issued on ___ and expired on ___”).
  4. File it in Monroe County Family Court (in person or online) and ask the judge for a new Temporary Order the same day if you need protection now (§ 828).
  5. Free service + hearing, same as a first filing.
Your next step: in the worksheet's incident log, add the most recent thing that happened — even if it was “just” a message. That entry is the heart of a re-filing.

They broke the order — reporting a violation

💲

Cost: $0. Calling the police is free, filing a violation petition is free, and you don't need a lawyer. You have two tracks at once — police and Family Court — and you can use both.

The law, simply

Breaking an Order of Protection is a crime, even if no one is physically hurt — a text or showing up can be enough if the order forbids contact. Police can arrest for criminal contempt: Penal Law § 215.50 (2nd degree, a Class A misdemeanor, up to 364 days in jail), and violations involving threats, physical contact, weapons, or repeat offenses can be felonies under §§ 215.51–215.52. Separately, in Family Court you can file a violation petition under Family Court Act § 846; if the judge finds after a hearing that the person willfully disobeyed, the court can jail them for up to 6 months per violation (§ 846-a), extend or strengthen the order, or both. Note: the order binds the respondent — you, the protected person, cannot “violate” your own order, and their claim that you “invited” contact is not a defense.

The form

GF-8 Petition — Violation of Order of Protection. It asks: which order (date/docket), which condition was broken, and exactly what they did, when, and where — plus what you want the court to do.

Steps

  1. If it's happening now or you're unsafe, call 911. Tell the officer there is an Order of Protection — they can verify it in the statewide registry and arrest.
  2. Save everything: screenshots of texts/calls (with dates visible), voicemails, photos, names of witnesses. Don't delete the thread.
  3. Write the violation into your incident log below while it's fresh — one entry per violation.
  4. File GF-8 in the Family Court that issued the order (in person or online). Keep a copy of the order with you — police and clerks will ask for the details on it.
  5. Go to the violation hearing. Bring your evidence. If the DA is also prosecuting criminal contempt, both cases can move forward at the same time.
Your next step: screenshot the violation with the date showing, then log it in the worksheet. Then decide: police report, GF-8, or both — both is allowed.

In California it's called a Domestic Violence Restraining Order (DVRO), under the Domestic Violence Prevention Act (Family Code starting at §6200). Pick the path that matches your situation — the form changes depending on which it is. Every path is free.

1 · First filing — asking the court for a DVRO

💲

Cost: $0. There is no filing fee for a domestic violence restraining order in California, no lawyer required, and the sheriff serves the papers for free.

The law, simply

A DVRO protects you from abuse by a spouse or ex-spouse, someone you live or lived with, a dating partner, the other parent of your child, or a close relative (Family Code §§ 6200, 6211, 6320). “Abuse” includes hurting or trying to hurt you, threats, stalking, and disturbing your peace. A judge reviews your request typically the same day or within 24 hours and can grant a Temporary Restraining Order (DV-110) right away, without the other person there. Your hearing is set within about 21 to 25 days.

2 · Renew it — your order is still active but the end date is coming

💲

Cost: $0. No fee to renew. File DV-700 Request to Renew Restraining Order + DV-710 Notice of Hearing to Renew during the 3 months before your order expires — and always before it expires. Once it lapses it cannot be renewed; a brand-new DV-100 request must be filed instead.

Under Family Code § 6345, you do not need to prove new abuse to renew. A renewed order can last 5 or more years, or permanently, at the judge's discretion — and your current order stays in effect until the renewal hearing.

4 · They broke the order — reporting a violation

💲

Cost: $0. Calling the police is free. Do both: call the police and file a violation report with the court.

The law, simply

Knowingly breaking a restraining order is a crime under Penal Code § 273.6, and police must arrest when they have probable cause (Penal Code § 836). A first offense is a misdemeanor: up to 1 year in county jail and a $1,000 fine. A repeat violation within 7 years involving violence or a credible threat can be charged as a felony, up to 3 years.

  2. Save everything: screenshots with dates, voicemails, photos, witness names. Don't delete the thread.
  3. Log the violation in your incident log below while it's fresh — one entry per violation.
  4. File a violation report with the court that issued your order, and keep a copy of the order with you.

The law, in plain words

Every topic below is the same pattern: what it means for you, in everyday language — then the actual New York law, cited, so you can check it or show a clerk, advocate, or lawyer exactly what you're talking about.

What is an Order of Protection, legally? +

It's a court order signed by a judge that tells a specific person what they must or must not do — stay away from you, stop contacting you, leave a shared home, stay away from your children, and more. Breaking it is a crime. In New York there is no single “restraining order” — Orders of Protection come from Family Court, Criminal Court, or Supreme Court (usually inside a divorce).

The law

Family Court Act § 842 lists the conditions a Family Court judge can put in an order. Orders are also entered in the statewide registry under Executive Law § 221-a, which is how police anywhere in New York can verify yours.

Family offenses — what counts (Article 8) +

Family Court can only act if what happened matches one of the “family offenses” in the law. In plain words, the list covers: hitting or trying to hurt someone; choking or blocking breathing; threats that make you fear harm; following, watching, or repeatedly contacting you to frighten you (stalking); repeated unwanted calls, texts, or messages meant to alarm or annoy (harassment); breaking your property; sexual contact without consent; forcing you to do something by threats (coercion); stealing your identity or money in certain cases; and posting intimate images of you without permission.

The law

Family Court Act § 812(1) gives Family Court and Criminal Courts shared (“concurrent”) jurisdiction over acts that would constitute: disorderly conduct; harassment 1st & 2nd degree; aggravated harassment 2nd degree; stalking 1st–4th degree; criminal mischief; menacing 2nd & 3rd degree; reckless endangerment; criminal obstruction of breathing or blood circulation; strangulation 1st & 2nd degree; assault 2nd & 3rd degree and attempted assault; sexual misconduct; forcible touching; sexual abuse 2nd & 3rd degree; identity theft 1st–3rd degree; grand larceny 3rd & 4th degree; coercion 2nd degree and coercion 3rd degree (subdivisions 1–3); and unlawful dissemination or publication of an intimate image (Penal Law definitions apply to each).

Emotional abuse and controlling behavior are real and dangerous — but on their own they may not match a listed offense. An advocate at Willow or Legal Aid can help you describe what happened in a way the court can act on. Never exaggerate — describe exactly what happened; the details usually fit the list better than people expect.

Who can ask Family Court for one? +

You qualify if the other person is: your current or former spouse; someone you have a child with; a relative by blood or marriage; someone you live or lived with as family; or someone you are or were in a dating/intimate relationship with (it does not have to have been sexual, and you don't have to have lived together). A roommate or casual acquaintance usually does not qualify for Family Court — but Criminal Court may still be an option if a crime was committed.

The law

Family Court Act § 812(1)(a)–(e) defines “members of the same family or household.” For an “intimate relationship,” judges weigh the type of relationship, how often you interacted, and how long it lasted (§ 812(1)(e)).

Family Court vs. Criminal Court — what's the difference? +

Family Court is the one you start, for free, by filing a petition. It's civil: the goal is protection, and you must show the offense is “more likely than not.” You don't need police involvement. Criminal Court starts when police make an arrest and the District Attorney prosecutes a crime; the judge there can also issue an Order of Protection, but you don't control that case and the standard of proof is higher (“beyond a reasonable doubt”). You can have both at the same time — they don't cancel each other out.

The law

Concurrent jurisdiction: Family Court Act § 812(1). Family Court burden of proof (“fair preponderance of the evidence”): § 832. A Family Court case is civil and its purpose is “to stop the violence, end the family disruption and obtain protection”: § 812(2)(b). One practical difference: a Criminal Court order cannot be extended after it expires — a Family Court order can be extended on good cause before it expires (§ 842).

✓ Checked Nov 5, 2026 — Family Court Act §§ 812, 832, 842 · WomensLaw.org · NY Courts agree.
Temporary vs. final orders +

A Temporary Order is emergency protection a judge can grant the same day you file, based on your petition alone, before the other person is heard. It lasts until your hearing date (courts usually set that within about two weeks, and it can be continued). It only becomes enforceable once the other person is served with it. A final Order comes after the hearing, where both sides can be heard — that's the longer order.

After hours: if Family Court is closed and you need protection right away, police can help you reach a Criminal Court judge who can issue a short Family Court temporary order on a form called GF-5b. It lasts no more than 4 days — you must go to Family Court before it runs out.

The law

Temporary orders: Family Court Act § 828. You have the right to file and be heard the same day or the next day Family Court is open: § 154 (see also § 153-c). After-hours temporary orders through Criminal Court use Form GF-5b.

How long does a final order last? +

Usually up to 2 years. If the judge finds “aggravating circumstances” — things like a weapon being used, serious injury, threats with a weapon, or prior violations — the order can last up to 5 years. The same is true if the conduct violated an earlier valid order. Check the expiration date printed on yours; that's the date your extension deadline is counted from.

The law

Family Court Act § 842: an order “shall set forth reasonable conditions of behavior to be observed for a period not in excess of two years … or … five years” on a finding of aggravating circumstances (defined in § 827(a)(vii)) or a finding that the conduct violated a valid order of protection.

✓ Checked Nov 5, 2026 — Family Court Act § 842 · WomensLaw.org · NY Courts agree.
Two kinds of orders: “stay away” vs. “refrain from” +

A full (“stay away”) order means no contact at all — no calls, texts, DMs, notes, gifts, showing up, or messages passed through friends or family — and staying a set distance from you, your home, work, school, and your children. A limited (“refrain from”) order lets the person be around you (some families need this for shared housing or kids) but forbids them from harassing, threatening, assaulting, or intimidating you. Even under a limited order, abusive behavior is a violation. You can ask for the full order; the judge decides.

The law

Conditions a judge may impose are listed in Family Court Act § 842, including staying away from the home, school, business, or place of employment of protected persons, and refraining from family offenses. Orders can also address temporary custody and safe visitation, and can require surrender of firearms in qualifying cases.

Extending, re-filing, and violations — the rules in one place +

Extend (before it expires): motion GF-10 + affirmation GF-10a, showing “good cause.” No new incident is required, and the judge can't deny it just because things have been quiet. Re-file (after it expires or a dismissal): a new Form 8-2 petition; lead with new incidents or ongoing threats and include the history. Violation (they broke a live order): call police (criminal contempt) and/or file GF-8 in Family Court; a willful violation can mean up to 6 months in jail per violation, plus a longer or stronger order.

The law

Extension: Family Court Act § 842 (“good cause”; quiet period alone is not sufficient ground to deny). Violation petitions and consequences: §§ 846, 846-a. Criminal contempt: Penal Law § 215.50 (Class A misdemeanor, up to 364 days), § 215.51 (Class E felony, up to 4 years), § 215.52 (aggravated, Class D felony, up to 7 years).

What happens after you file

The same basic sequence for every path. Timelines vary — the court gives you exact dates in writing.

  1. The court reviews and you may see a judge the same day

    For a new or re-filed petition, the judge can issue a Temporary Order of Protection right away (§ 828) based on your sworn petition. For an extension motion or violation petition, you'll get a court date.

  2. The papers are served — free

    A sheriff, police officer, or court-arranged server hands the petition and any temporary order to the respondent. The order takes effect once served. You don't do this part, and you shouldn't try.

  3. The hearing

    Both sides can tell their story, bring witnesses and evidence, and have a lawyer (free if you qualify). In Family Court you must show the offense is more likely than not (§ 832). For a violation, the court must find the disobedience was willful (§ 846).

  4. The judge decides

    Final order (up to 2 years, or 5 with aggravating circumstances, § 842), an extension for a reasonable period, consequences for a violation (up to 6 months in jail per willful violation, § 846-a) — or dismissal if the proof isn't there. You get the decision and paperwork in writing.

  5. Live with the order — and log everything

    Keep a copy of the order with you and one at home, work, and your children's school. Keep logging any contact or violations here or on paper — that log is gold if you ever need to extend, re-file, or report a violation.

  6. Before it expires

    Set the reminders this page builds (30 / 14 / 7 days out). Extending before the end date is far easier than re-filing after it.

Hearing prep — walk in ready

The hearing is where the judge decides. It is usually short, and judges in Family Court do this every day. Here's what to bring, what will happen, and how to practice telling your story.

✓ Bring-list and hearing steps checked Nov 5, 2026 — NY Courts guidance · WomensLaw.org · court-practice guides agree (practical guidance, not a statute).

Bring this

  • Photo ID
  • Your copy of the petition and the Temporary Order
  • Your file / docket number (it's on all your paperwork — the clerk and judge will use it)
  • A written, dated timeline of incidents — your incident log from Step 1c, printed
  • Printed evidence, organized oldest-to-newest: texts and emails (dates visible), voicemails saved, photos of injuries or damage with the date written on each, police reports, medical records, and any prior orders
  • Witness names and phone numbers — and the witnesses themselves, if they'll come
  • Two copies of everything printed: one for the judge, one for you (the court may also need one for the other side)

What to expect in the room

  • Both sides testify under oath. You'll be sworn in — just tell the truth, in your own words.
  • You go first as the petitioner: what happened, when, where — the judge may ask questions as you go.
  • The respondent gets to respond and tell their side, and can bring a lawyer. You don't have to argue with them — speak to the judge, not to them.
  • The judge decides by “more likely than not” (a fair preponderance of the evidence, § 832) — not the criminal standard. Your dated, specific facts and printed evidence are what carry weight.
  • Free lawyer: if you want one and can't afford one, ask the judge for assigned counsel — you can ask at any appearance, including this one.
  • Don't miss it. If you don't show up, your case can be dismissed. Running late or unsafe to travel? Call the clerk (585-371-3544) as early as you can and say so.

Practice script — telling the judge what happened

Judges listen for short, specific, dated sentences — one fact per sentence. The pattern is: date → place → what they did or said → what happened next. Like this:

Say it like this

“Your Honor, my name is ____. I'm asking for an order of protection against ____, my former partner. On March 3rd, at my apartment, he came to my door at 11 p.m. and said, ‘I will kill you if you leave.’ I called 911. The officers took a report. On March 9th, he sent me 42 texts in one night. I have printed them, with the dates showing. I am afraid he will hurt me, and I'm asking the Court to order him to stay away from me and my children.”

Tips: stick to what you saw and heard — exact words in quotes if you remember them. Skip opinions (“he's crazy”) and give facts (“he punched the wall next to my head”). If you don't remember a date exactly, say “around” the date — don't guess under oath. If you get emotional, pause and breathe; judges expect it and will wait.

The law, in plain words — California

What it means for you, in everyday language — then the actual California law, cited, so you can check it or show a clerk, advocate, or lawyer exactly what you're talking about.

What is a Domestic Violence Restraining Order, legally? +

It's a court order signed by a judge telling a specific person what they must or must not do — stay away from you, stop contacting you, move out of a shared home, stay away from your children, and more. Breaking it is a crime, even if nobody is physically hurt — a forbidden text can be enough.

The law

The Domestic Violence Prevention Act, Family Code §§ 6200 et seq. What a judge can order — including stay-away and no-contact orders — is in § 6320 and related sections.

Who can ask for one? +

You qualify if the person who abused you is: your spouse or ex-spouse; someone you live or lived with; someone you are or were dating; the other parent of your child; or a close relative (parent, child, sibling, grandparent, in-law).

The law

Family Code § 6211 defines who is protected under the Domestic Violence Prevention Act; “abuse” is defined in § 6203 and includes disturbing another party's peace under § 6320.

Temporary vs. after-hearing orders +

A Temporary Restraining Order (DV-110) is emergency protection a judge can grant typically the same day or within 24 hours of filing, based on your papers alone. It lasts until your court hearing, which is set within about 21 to 25 days. It protects you once the other person has been served with it. The other person must be served at least 5 days before the hearing — by the sheriff (free) or by any adult who is not part of the case. You cannot serve the papers yourself. An order after hearing (DV-130) comes after both sides are heard.

The law

Temporary orders and hearing timelines: Family Code §§ 240, 6320–6327, 6345; service and proof of service use form DV-200. At the hearing the judge decides whether the abuse is “more likely than not.”

How long does an order after hearing last? +

Up to 5 years. If the judge does not write an expiration date, the order lasts 3 years by default. Check the date printed on your DV-130 — your renewal deadline (3 months before it) is counted from that date.

The law

Family Code § 6345: an order after hearing may last up to five years; if no expiration date is stated, it expires three years after issuance.

✓ Checked Nov 5, 2026 — Family Code § 6345 · California Courts · WomensLaw.org California agree.
Renewing, re-filing, and violations — the rules in one place +

Renew (before it expires): file DV-700 + DV-710 during the 3 months before expiration. No new abuse needs to be proven. The renewed order can last 5 or more years, or permanently. Re-file (after it expires): it cannot be renewed once lapsed — file a brand-new DV-100. Violation: call the police and file a violation report with the court; knowingly breaking the order is a crime.

The law

Renewal: Family Code § 6345. Violation crime: Penal Code § 273.6 (first offense a misdemeanor — up to 1 year county jail, $1,000 fine; a repeat within 7 years with violence or a credible threat can be a felony, up to 3 years). Mandatory arrest on probable cause: Penal Code § 836.

✓ Checked Nov 5, 2026 — Family Code § 6345 · Penal Code §§ 273.6, 836 · California Courts agree.
